*El espectro de la voz* (The Spectrum of the Voice) is an interactive installation that came out of two UNTREF research teams working together: AudioStellar and Intercambios Transorgánicos. We used the flow of people through the yearly Tecnópolis fair to collect visitors' voices, because we are interested in the voice as sound material, as raw material and as a problem.

We focus on the traits of identity that show up in our voices: our angry voice is not the same as our bored, worried, excited or relaxed voice, and at the same time each person's voice has complex, changing qualities. In our research on the voice we found that speech therapy tends to analyze and classify voices by gender, or by the ideas of health and illness. The basic question of the experiment was: what happens when we group voices by something that is neither gender nor health and illness? Our working hypothesis was that grouping them by their variations in frequency, spectrum, amplitude and length, at the level of the phrase, the word and the phoneme, could produce groupings different from those of culture and medicine.

The visit began with a walk through the research of both teams, including Intercambios Transorgánicos' projects [Archivoz](archivoz.html) and [Fonocosa](fonocosa.html): images, code, records of interfaces, notes and videos on the walls, with voices playing as sound in the room. It ended in the space of the experiment.

There, mediators invited visitors to record their voices anonymously, following an activation protocol we wrote for them. It started with a rehearsal, with nothing recorded: the mediator showed the page of words and asked "Which of these words do you say most?", "How do you say this one?", "How do you say hello when you get home?" Then each visitor said every word once, the way they would say it, which is not the same as reading it. Finally, they chose three words and said each one in at least two different ways. The mediators never gave the words an expression of their own. The idea was for each visitor to remember, out loud and from their own vocal memory, how they say everyday expressions of our regional vocal culture, playing with the words until their voice came out more sincere and uninhibited. If someone read them flatly, with no change of tone, that was part of the experiment too.

The recordings were processed and shown in [AudioStellar](https://audiostellar.xyz/), free software for sound experimentation that uses artificial intelligence to place audio files in a two-dimensional space: points close together are spectrally similar sounds, with similar timbres, and points far apart are different sounds. The voices gathered week after week formed a constellation that grew with every visit. On the largest screen, visitors explored it with their bodies: a camera followed their movements, building on the sensing system of the [C3D](c3d.html), and their gestures played the clusters of voices.

The experiment also built a bank of voices for the research of both teams.
